The blocking effect

Report these two to the class tally

Why it happens

Look at what you actually saw. One food caused reactions from the very start. Later, a new food was quietly added alongside it — and every one of those meals still caused a reaction. But the new food never got the chance to prove itself: its partner already explained everything.

The prediction-error account (Rescorla–Wagner)

Learning tracks surprise, not just pairing. When the blocked food first appeared, its partner already predicted the reaction — so the outcome was fully expected. No surprise, no prediction error, almost nothing learned about the new food (ΔV ≈ 0). In the control pair, both foods were new, so the reaction was a genuine surprise and the associative strength got split between them.

This is exactly the result that broke simple contiguity theories: two foods can be paired with an outcome equally often, yet be learned very differently. Kamin (1969); Rescorla & Wagner (1972); human allergy paradigm after Dickinson, Shanks & Evenden (1984).
